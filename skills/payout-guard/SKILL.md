description: Audits a self-service "update my payout destination" flow — direct deposit, vendor ACH/wire details, marketplace seller payout — for the gap that lets a compromised login turn into stolen money. Does changing the payout destination require re-authentication specific to this action, does it hold the change for a cooling-off period before the new destination starts receiving funds, and is the notification of the change sent through a channel the same compromise couldn't also control (not just an email inside the same SSO session the attacker already owns)? Use when adding or reviewing a payroll/vendor-payment/marketplace "change my bank account" or "update payment details" endpoint, or when asked "does changing payout details need a hold period", "can a phished login redirect someone's paycheck", "do we notify outside the compromised channel when bank details change", or "audit our payout-destination-change flow".

# payout-guard

A payout destination field looks like any other profile setting — an account number, a routing number, a form that saves on submit — but changing it doesn't just update a record, it redirects money that hasn't been sent yet. The naive implementation gates it the way it gates a display-name edit: confirm there's an active session, write the new value, done. That treats "where does your paycheck go" as cosmetic, when it's actually one of the highest-leverage targets in the whole system — redirect it once and every future payout cycle pays the attacker instead of the real employee or vendor, with no transaction to reverse because nothing fraudulent happened at the payment layer, only at the destination-selection layer upstream of it.

This is not hypothetical, and it's not small. The FBI's IC3 PSA I-091019-PSA ("Business Email Compromise: The $26 Billion Scam," Sept 2019) logged 1,053 complaints of exactly this payroll-diversion pattern between January 2018 and June 2019 — direct-deposit change requests up over 800% in that window, $8.3M in reported losses from that slice alone. More recently, Microsoft's "Storm-2657" / "Payroll Pirate" campaign (reported Oct 2025, corroborated by The Register, BleepingComputer, The Hacker News, and others) hit US universities by phishing SSO credentials (AiTM, no MFA step-up required on the target account), logging into Workday, and changing direct-deposit details directly — then hiding the system's own "your payment details changed" notification behind an inbox rule the attacker set up using the same compromised session. That last detail is the part a naive port of "just notify them" misses: the notification channel and the compromised channel were the same one.

## Step 1: does changing the payout destination require proving identity again, for this action specifically?

Find the handler and check what it actually demands beyond an active session: current password, a step-up MFA challenge, or nothing beyond "you're logged in"? Same question `email-guard` asks of an email-change endpoint, and the answer matters the same way here — a live session proves someone authenticated at some point, not that they're still the account owner right now. If there's no re-authentication gate on this specific action, that's the finding, not "does the app have auth" (it does, trivially).

## Step 2: is the notification channel actually independent of the channel that could already be compromised?

This is where payout-guard diverges from a generic "notify the old channel" check, and it's the step most implementations get wrong by analogy to email-change flows. An email-change flow's old-address notification works as a safety net *because* the old mailbox is a priori a separate credential from whatever got stolen to hijack the session. A payout-change flow inside an SSO-federated HR/payroll system doesn't have that separation: if the attacker phished the SSO session (the Storm-2657 shape), they're very often already standing inside the same mailbox that notification would land in, or can set an inbox rule to bury it the moment it arrives — exactly what happened in the real campaign. Check: does the "your payout details changed" notice go only to the email tied to the same SSO identity that was just used to make the change, or does it also reach a channel that identity doesn't control — an SMS to a phone number on file, a physical letter, a required callback to a number already on record? Email-only notification inside the same identity perimeter the attacker is already inside of is not an independent check, even though it looks like one in the code.

## Step 3: is there a hold/cooling-off period before the new destination actually starts receiving money?

Even a perfect notification doesn't help if the new destination is already live and the next payout cycle has already run by the time anyone reads it. Check whether the system holds the account on the *old* destination for some window after the change is submitted — long enough for a real owner to notice the notification from Step 2 and object — or switches over immediately and treats the notice as an FYI after the fact. "We send a notification" and "the old destination stays live until someone can act on that notification" are different claims; confirm which one the code actually does, and if there's a hold, whether reverting during it is a user-initiated self-service action or something that needs a support ticket (a hold nobody can act on alone doesn't help the person traveling or asleep when the fraudulent change goes through).

## Report

BLOCK for: no re-authentication required for this specific action beyond an existing session; a change that takes effect (new destination starts receiving funds) with no hold period at all; or a notification sent only through a channel tied to the same identity/session that made the change, with no independent channel reached. CAUTION for: re-authentication and a hold period both present, but the only notification channel is still inside the same SSO/identity perimeter (so a sufficiently capable attacker who owns that perimeter could still suppress it, even though a less-capable one couldn't), or the hold period exists but reverting during it requires a manual process slow enough to miss the next payout cycle anyway. SAFE only with evidence: step-up re-authentication gates the action, a hold period keeps the old destination live past the point a real owner could plausibly see and act on the notice, and that notice reaches a channel genuinely outside the identity perimeter used to make the change.

## Boundaries

- Not `email-guard`'s territory — that skill shares the step-up/notify/hold shape but for a login/recovery email, where old-address notification is a priori independent of whatever hijacked the session (stolen cookie, XSS). This skill is about a payout destination, and the whole point of Step 2 above is that the "independent channel" assumption email-guard can lean on often doesn't hold here, because the realistic attacker path (AiTM-phished SSO) can already be standing inside the notification channel itself.
- Not `rotation-guard`'s territory — that skill audits an *operational* credential (DB password, API key, Vault lease) reaching every service consumer after rotation. This skill is about a person's own payout destination being changed by someone who may not be them, a different actor and a different kind of change entirely.
- Doesn't extend to the payment rail itself — once a legitimate-looking ACH/wire transfer is initiated to a destination the system believes is correctly authorized, clawing it back is a banking/compliance problem this skill doesn't address. It audits only the destination-selection step upstream of the transfer, where the fraud actually happens.
- Doesn't require every system to add SMS or postal-mail notification if neither is already collected — the finding in that case is "the only available channel is inside the same identity perimeter the attacker could already own," not "go integrate SMS." Name the gap against what identity data the system actually has, don't invent a channel it doesn't collect.
- Can't confirm from source alone that a notification is actually delivered and read, or that a support team enforcing a manual hold-period revert actually responds inside the window — it audits whether the code's logic holds the destination and attempts the right notification, and says so plainly rather than claiming an outcome it can't observe.
