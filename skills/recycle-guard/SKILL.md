# recycle-guard

A phone number isn't a stable identifier the way an email address mostly is — carriers reclaim numbers that go dormant (a cancelled line, an unpaid account, a port-out with no port-in) and reassign them to new subscribers on an ordinary, unremarkable timescale, not as any kind of breach. An app that binds SMS-based 2FA or password recovery to a phone number at enrollment and then never asks the question again is trusting a fact that quietly stops being true. When the number gets recycled, its new legitimate owner — someone who did nothing wrong, just signed up for new service — starts receiving the previous owner's OTP codes and recovery links. If that previous owner's password is already sitting in a breach dump (overwhelmingly likely for any account old enough for its number to have gone dormant and been recycled), the new owner now holds everything needed to take the account over: a leaked password plus the "something you have" factor the app itself handed them.

This is grounded in actual measurement, not a hypothetical: Lee & Narayanan (Princeton), *"Security and Privacy Risks of Number Recycling at Mobile Carriers,"* IEEE eCrime 2021 — 259 recycled numbers sampled from T-Mobile and Verizon, 83% found vulnerable to account hijack or PII exposure through at least one online service still bound to that number, and 100 of the 259 independently tied to credentials already circulating in breach dumps. The same paper found carrier-provided reverse number-lookup tools with no rate limit or query cap, making it trivial for an attacker to check which of a batch of just-recycled numbers are worth targeting before spending a dollar renting any of them. The vulnerability isn't in the carrier's reassignment — that's expected, normal behavior for a finite number space — it's in every app downstream that treats "controls this phone number" as a fact fixed at signup instead of a fact that can silently change owners.

## Step 1: find whether phone ownership is ever re-verified after the initial binding

Locate every code path that binds a phone number to an account — SMS 2FA enrollment, recovery-channel setup, a "add a phone number" account-settings action — and trace what happens *after* that first verification:

- Is there any periodic or event-triggered re-verification of the bound number, or is the one OTP sent at enrollment the only proof of ownership the app ever collects for that number's entire lifetime on the account?
- Does the app query (or could it cheaply query) any signal that the number's status changed since binding — a carrier lookup API (Twilio Lookup's line-type/carrier fields, a porting-status check) that would reveal a recent port or reassignment — or does it have zero visibility into the phone network side at all?
- If the account itself goes dormant (no login, no app activity) for a long stretch, does anything re-check the phone binding specifically when activity resumes, or does a returning "owner" (who might now be the number's new legitimate holder, not the original account owner) just walk straight back in via "forgot password → SMS code"?

## Step 2: find what happens when a number legitimately gets reassigned inside the app's own data model

A recycled number isn't only a risk through the original app that bound it — it's a risk the moment *any* second person tries to bind that same number to a *different* account, because that's the app's one clear, in-band signal that the number has changed hands:

- When a new signup or phone-change attempt uses a number already bound to an existing account, what does the app do — block the new user outright (locking out the legitimate new owner of a number they fairly acquired, with no path forward), silently allow a second binding (ambiguous OTP delivery, delivered to whichever session asks first), or actually walk the new owner through proving ownership and then unbinding the number from the old account's 2FA/recovery (the only outcome that's both correct and doesn't just relocate the vulnerability)?
- Is unbinding the old account's phone-based 2FA/recovery, when it happens, accompanied by any notice to the old account through a channel that doesn't depend on the number — email, if one's on file — so the original owner isn't silently downgraded to a single factor (or locked out of recovery entirely) with no idea it happened?
- For an account with both email and phone as recovery options, does losing the phone-based path (via this re-binding) ever leave the account with *zero* working recovery channels because the email on file is itself stale — and does the app surface that state anywhere, or does it just fail silently the next time recovery is attempted?

## Step 3: check how costly it actually is for an attacker to find a worthwhile target

- Does anything in the app's own flows (a public "is this number registered" check on signup, a recovery flow that confirms whether a number has an account before sending an OTP) leak which recycled numbers are worth acquiring, independent of the carrier-side reverse-lookup exposure the Princeton paper measured?
- Is there rate limiting or monitoring on recovery/2FA attempts keyed by phone number, so that someone who did acquire a genuinely recycled number and is probing it against the OTP/recovery flow leaves a detectable trail — or does the flow look identical to an ordinary user who just got a new phone?

## Report

Lead with whichever applies: (a) the app never re-verifies a bound phone number after initial enrollment and has no signal for number reassignment at all — cite the enrollment code path and confirm there's no re-check anywhere downstream; or (b) re-binding to a new account exists but doesn't safely unbind (or notify) the old account, relocating the vulnerability instead of closing it. BLOCK for a flow where a second, legitimate binding attempt either silently grants dual access or unbinds the old account with no notification sent anywhere the old owner would see it — this is the exact mechanic the Princeton measurement shows is already being exploited at scale, not a theoretical gap. CAUTION for re-binding that's handled safely but leaves the *original* account owner's notice depending on an email address that might itself be stale, or for an app with no periodic re-verification but also no SMS-only recovery path (phone is 2FA only, with a non-phone-dependent recovery route that still works). SAFE only with evidence: either periodic/event-driven re-verification of phone ownership actually exists, or binding conflicts are resolved by provably re-verifying the new claimant and then safely revoking and notifying the old account — not just "we've never had a complaint."

## Boundaries

- Not `dormancy-guard`'s territory — that skill audits whether an *account*-level inactivity sweep's activity signal covers non-interactive usage (API keys, webhooks, service accounts). This skill is about a *phone number*'s ownership drifting out from under an account that might still be perfectly active; the two can compound (a dormant account is exactly the kind whose bound number is most likely to have been recycled by the time anyone returns to it) but neither audits the other's mechanic.
- Not `email-guard`'s territory — that skill audits the application-side email/phone-*change* endpoint itself (re-authentication, old-address notification, confirm-before-activate) for a user actively changing their own recovery channel, and explicitly scopes out carrier-side SIM swapping as a telecom-network problem outside an app's code. This skill is the inverse failure mode: no one changed anything in the app at all — the carrier silently reassigned the physical number out from under a binding the app made once and never revisited. A review that touches both an active change flow and a dormant unverified binding should flag each under the matching skill.
- Not `push-guard`'s territory — that skill audits a push-notification *device token's* lifecycle (logout, token refresh, provider-reported-dead tokens). A phone number and a push token are both "a channel the app trusts a device/person controls," but they fail differently: a push token usually gets explicitly reissued or reported dead by the provider, while a recycled phone number changes hands with no signal to the app at all unless this skill's Step 1 checks turn one up.
- Doesn't audit carrier-side number-reassignment policy, porting fraud (an attacker tricking a carrier into porting a number they don't own — that's SIM-swap territory, out of scope for both this skill and `email-guard`), or telecom reverse-lookup rate limiting — those sit on the carrier's side of a boundary this skill can't see past; it only audits what the application does with a phone number once it has one.
- Doesn't design a replacement recovery scheme from scratch (WebAuthn, TOTP, recovery codes) — if the Report's SAFE bar can't be met because SMS is the *only* recovery/2FA factor with no non-phone fallback, say so plainly as part of the finding rather than prescribing a specific replacement architecture.
